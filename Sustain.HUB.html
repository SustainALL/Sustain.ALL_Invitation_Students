Section 1

Project Overview

"The resources for circular campus transformation often already exist. The furniture repository and unused cafeterias were there before this project started. What was missing was a process to connect them." — Key learning from the Sustain.ALL project journey

This project was developed as part of the Sustain.ALL Transformation HUB at TU Berlin during the summer semester of 2026. It addresses two interconnected challenges: the existence of unused campus spaces and the persistence of linear furniture procurement — despite existing surplus resources held in TU Berlin's own furniture repository.

The project proposes transforming two unused cafeterias at TU Berlin into flexible Circular Co-Creation Hubs, furnished entirely with refurbished furniture from TU Berlin's own furniture repository. Students are involved throughout as co-designers, makers, and space hosts.

The Spaces — Site Visit Documentation

Two unused cafeteria spaces at TU Berlin were identified by campus partner Michael and visited as part of this project. The photographs below were taken during the site visits and document the current condition and potential of both spaces.

Cafeteria 1 — Outdoor / Terrace Area

🔍 Cafeteria 1 — Site Observations Large terrace/outdoor-covered area with existing tables and bench seating. Glass facade on three sides provides excellent natural light. Existing furniture (folding tables, beer garden benches) is in varying condition — some items suitable for refurbishment and modular reconfiguration. Space has strong potential for casual, social, community-oriented programming.

Cafeteria 2 — Indoor Space (Math Building)

🔍 Cafeteria 2 — Site Observations Large elongated interior space with high ceilings and characteristic blue structural beams. Excellent natural light from skylights and floor-to-ceiling windows on both sides. Existing furniture (tables, stackable chairs, benches) is already present and suitable for circular transformation. Tropical plants and existing track lighting create a distinctive, warm atmosphere that is significantly better than typical TU Berlin rooms. Strong potential as a flagship co-creation space.
Section 3

The Challenge

Core Research Question How can TU Berlin reconnect unused spaces, reusable furniture, and student needs to create circular co-creation hubs that support sustainability transformation?

The Problem at Its Root

TU Berlin operates on a largely linear model: spaces go unused, surplus furniture is stored, and new furniture is still purchased — despite existing surplus. Students consistently report a lack of attractive informal spaces to collaborate, meet, and work between classes. The challenge is not primarily technical or regulatory. It is fundamentally about failing the student experience:

🚫 Student-Facing Root Causes

  • Unappealing atmosphere — nothing draws students in
  • Old, uninspiring furniture — actively discourages staying
  • Low awareness — most students don't know these spaces exist
  • Mismatch with needs — no flexible, social, well-equipped layout

🏛️ Institutional Root Causes

  • Institutional silos — space management, procurement, and sustainability are not coordinated
  • Linear procurement default — no reuse-first process exists
  • No furniture inventory system — reuse opportunities remain invisible
  • Lack of student agency — students excluded from space decisions
Section 4

Research Evidence & Theoretical Framing

Circular Economy (CE)

A systemic approach keeping materials in use through reuse, repair, and refurbishment. Applied to campus furniture procurement, modular design, and space management. [Mendoza et al., 2019]

Design for Disassembly (DfD)

Mechanical joints only — all components must be separable without damage. Enables circular end-of-life from day one and is a hard design rule for this project. [ISO 20887:2020]

Hidden Curriculum (Mendoza et al., 2018)

Universities teach CE but rarely practice it in their own operations. This project directly closes that gap at TU Berlin by making the campus the living lab.

In-Between Spaces (Akkurt & Akbulut, 2025)

Transitional campus spaces are critical for informal learning and collaboration yet consistently overlooked in campus planning despite their potential.

Living Lab (Evans et al., 2015)

TU Berlin campus as a real-world experimentation environment where students co-design and test circular innovations in their own everyday environment.

LCA-by-Design

Prospective LCA in Phase 2 sets the environmental baseline; real LCA in Phase 4 measures actual outcomes. Produces a publishable dataset comparing forecast vs. actual CO₂ savings.

Section 5

The Proposed Framework

🏗️

Adaptive Reuse

Two unused cafeterias are reactivated for collaborative use without major structural modifications, preserving existing infrastructure.

🔧

Modular Furniture Transformation

Furniture from the repository is assessed, refurbished, and transformed into modular, demountable systems — mechanical joints only, no adhesives.

🤝

Participatory Co-Design

Students shape every stage through surveys, co-design workshops, and Makeathons. Space Hosts maintain the hubs and activate community events.

The QR Code Transparency Layer

🏷️ "This furniture has a story." Each piece of refurbished furniture carries a QR code documenting its origin, refurbishment process, and estimated CO₂ savings. Rather than a simple label, it tells the story of each piece — making circular economy principles visible and tangible in everyday campus life. Research shows this kind of product storytelling strengthens public engagement with sustainability practices. [Bashir, 2022]

Why These Two Cafeterias?

As visible from the site visit photographs, both cafeterias have a central campus location, existing infrastructure (electricity, lighting, plumbing), and significant floor areas that can accommodate a wide range of activities without structural modifications. Their previous function as gathering spaces creates a natural connection to campus community life.

Cafeteria 2 in particular is a striking space — high ceilings, skylight windows, distinctive blue structural beams, and existing tropical plants — that already has the bones of a remarkable community space. It needs activation, not construction.

System Mapping

Circular Material Flow

1
Inventory

Furniture Repository Assessment

Survey surplus furniture: chairs, tables, shelving. Triage rule: mechanical joints only, no composites, all components separable without damage.

2
Design

Modular Unit Design + Prospective LCA

Architecture/Timber Construction students design the modular furniture system from compatible repository stock. Prospective LCA completed before construction.

3
Makeathon

Students + Craftsmen Build the Hub

Physical refurbishment and assembly. QR codes attached to each piece. First deployment and activation event at Cafeteria 1.

4
Operation

Cafeteria 1 Active → Rotation to Cafeteria 2

Space Hosts active. Real LCA data collected and compared against prospective forecast. Usage and community data gathered continuously.

5
Circular End-of-Life

Return to Repository or Permanent Adoption

Sort-specific disassembly. Furniture returns to repository or enters permanent use. Best-practice guide published for replication at other universities.

Section 9

Student Survey Results

A structured student survey was conducted as part of the project methodology to empirically understand students' current use of campus spaces, their needs, and their attitudes toward reused furniture and co-creation. 14 responses were collected from students across multiple TU Berlin faculties, primarily Civil Systems Engineering and Faculty VI.

Key Headline Findings

100%
of respondents would use a co-creation space in an unused cafeteria (50% very likely, 50% likely)
71%
love the idea of refurbished second-hand furniture — sustainability matters to them
79%
would be interested in joining a Makeathon workshop (21% definitely, 57% if timing works)
Q2 — Campus presence

79% of respondents are on campus 1–3 days per week, indicating high potential demand for drop-in collaborative spaces rather than daily regulars.

Q4 — Where students go informally

50% of students regularly leave campus entirely when they need informal space — a direct validation of the problem this project addresses.

Q6 — Likelihood to use a cafeteria co-creation space

100% of respondents said they would likely or very likely use the space. 14% didn't even know these cafeterias existed — confirming the awareness gap.

Q7 — Intended activities

Group project meetings (86%) and individual studying (79%) top the list — the space needs to support both quiet focus and collaborative work simultaneously.

Q8 — Missing space types on campus

Quiet phone/video call space (64%) and small group discussion rooms (57%) are the most urgently missing — both are achievable through modular furniture zoning.

Q9 — Attitude toward refurbished furniture

107% approval (71% love it + 36% fine if quality visible) — the main condition is that quality and story are communicated clearly. This is exactly what the QR code layer addresses.

Q11 — Interest in Makeathon / design workshop

79% open to participating in a Makeathon if timing works — a strong signal for the participatory co-design model.

Open-Ended Responses — What Students Said

💬 What would make you choose this space over a café?

  • "Somewhere flexible where I could be quiet when needed, or talk when needed"
  • "Good lighting and tables to work and plugin to charge batteries while working"
  • "Accessibility, enough seats and power outlets"
  • "Distance, Wi-Fi access, less formality"
  • "Easy access in campus, privacy for video calls"
  • "Having group meeting rooms"

💬 What should the space look like?

  • "Open, accessible and full of light spaces"
  • "Not too loud of a colour mix design wise, but still a distinct look from normal TU interior"
  • "The ability to change to host single students to a group study"
  • "Maybe free or reduced-price snacks — your current concept sounds really cool!"
  • "Classrooms not all occupied could be used for team meetings by booking"

Which buildings need better informal spaces? (Q13)

Students identified: Main Building, Civil Engineering Faculty, Faculty VI campus, Wedding Campus, and the campus as a whole. Multiple responses pointed to buildings beyond the main campus as particularly underserved — validating the need for a scalable, replicable model rather than a single fixed location.

Individual Reflection

By Helia Haghighipanah — Student ID: 0494334 — Civil Systems Engineering, TU Berlin

My Learning Journey

When I joined Sustain.ALL, I expected a course about sustainability concepts. What I actually experienced was something more challenging and more valuable: learning how to navigate real institutional systems to create change. The journey moved from abstract challenge framing to concrete site visits, from theoretical stakeholder maps to actual conversations with Michael inside a disused cafeteria.

The most significant shift in my understanding came when I realised that the root cause of the problem was not primarily technical or regulatory — it was experiential. The unused spaces fail students not because of fire safety codes but because they are unappealing, invisible, and mismatched with how students actually want to work and connect. That insight changed how I framed every subsequent step of the project.

My Personal Contributions

  • Developed the full research framework including literature review, stakeholder analysis, system mapping, and 5-phase roadmap
  • Designed the student survey and formulated interview guides for stakeholder conversations
  • Conducted site visits to both unused cafeterias with campus partner Michael
  • Wrote the formal project proposal integrating adaptive reuse, modular furniture transformation, and participatory design
  • Developed the LCA-by-Design methodology connecting prospective and real environmental assessment
  • Created the QR code storytelling concept ("This furniture has a story") as the communication layer
  • Collected and analysed the student survey, identifying key design requirements for the hub

Key Challenges & Lessons Learned

🔄 What I Would Do Differently

  • Engage Facility Management formally and earlier — not only through Michael's informal channel
  • Pilot the student survey with a small group before distribution to catch unclear questions earlier
  • Build more flexibility into the site visit timeline — coordinating across stakeholders takes longer than expected

💡 Key Surprises

  • Early contact with the right person (Michael) unlocked access that formal processes would have taken months to achieve
  • The furniture repository existed all along — the missing ingredient was simply a process to connect it to student needs
  • 100% of survey respondents said they would use a co-creation cafeteria space — the demand is unambiguous

How This Course Changed My Perspective on Sustainability

Before Sustain.ALL, I understood sustainability primarily through technical and engineering lenses — LCA methodologies, material efficiency, structural performance. This course showed me that the hardest part of sustainability is not the calculation but the conversation: engaging the right stakeholders at the right moment, navigating institutional cultures, and designing processes that people actually want to participate in.

Universities are uniquely positioned as both knowledge producers and operational actors. When they align what they teach with how they operate — using their own campuses as living labs — they become proof of concept for the transitions they advocate. This project is a small but concrete example of what that alignment looks like in practice. The furniture exists. The spaces exist. The student motivation exists. What was missing was a process to connect them — and building that process is what this project set out to do.

Transformation Insight Sustainability transformation is not primarily a technical problem. It is a coordination and culture problem. The resources for change were already there at TU Berlin. What was needed was someone willing to connect the dots across institutional silos and create a process where none existed before.
